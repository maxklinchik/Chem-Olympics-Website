Nitrate Pollution — The Chemistry

This project focuses on nitrate waste from common nitrogen fertilizers. The pollutant is simple but important: once nitrogen leaves the field as nitrate, it is highly soluble, easy to transport, and able to trigger eutrophication, oxygen loss, and drinking-water contamination. The main source chemicals are urea CO(NH2)2, ammonium nitrate NH4NO3, and ammonia NH3.

What the Waste Is and Where It Comes From

The waste form is mainly nitrate, NO3-, created when crops receive more nitrogen than they can absorb. Rain and irrigation water move the excess nitrogen into drainage systems and groundwater. Because nitrate carries a negative charge, it stays dissolved in water rather than binding strongly to soil particles.

Urea

Common dry fertilizer, CO(NH2)2, that converts to ammonium in moist soil.

Ammonium Nitrate

Highly soluble NH4NO3 that can move rapidly with runoff and leachate.

Ammonia

Anhydrous NH3 is injected into soil and quickly becomes ammonium in the presence of water.

Key Chemical Properties

High Solubility and Mobility

Nitrate is an ion, so it dissolves easily in water. That makes it highly mobile in soils, especially when rainfall, irrigation, or tile drainage moves water through the root zone. Unlike hydrophobic pollutants, nitrate does not stick strongly to soil organic matter, so it can travel into streams and aquifers.

Resistance to Removal in Oxygen-Poor Water

In oxygen-poor groundwater, nitrate can remain in solution for long periods because the natural denitrification process slows down. Where organic carbon and anaerobic microbes are available, nitrate can be reduced to nitrogen gas, but many aquifers lack the conditions needed for fast removal.

Residence Time

Nitrate does not have one fixed half-life because it is controlled by water movement, microbial activity, and geology. In shallow, oxygenated groundwater, nitrate can persist for months to years. In wetland soils, residence time is shorter because denitrifying bacteria can convert it to harmless nitrogen gas.

Important Reactions

Two reactions explain how fertilizer nitrogen becomes a contamination problem: urea hydrolysis and nitrification.

NO2- + 0.5O2 → NO3-

Nitrification is fastest in warm, moist, oxygen-rich soils. In saturated or anoxic soils, the pathway can shift to denitrification, where microbes reduce nitrate to N2 or N2O under low-oxygen conditions.

Denitrification in Wet Soils

NO3- → NO2- → N2O → N2 occurs under anoxic conditions, usually in wetlands, compacted soils, and saturated sediments. This is helpful for cleanup because it converts nitrate into nitrogen gas, but incomplete denitrification can release nitrous oxide, a potent greenhouse gas.

Related Nitrogen Forms

Ammonia and Ammonium

Ammonia is the reduced form of nitrogen commonly injected into fields. In water it equilibrates with ammonium, which is less mobile than nitrate but still contributes to nutrient loading. High ammonia levels can also be toxic to fish directly because unionized ammonia readily crosses gill membranes.

Nitrite

Nitrite (NO2-) is the short-lived intermediate between ammonium and nitrate. It is less stable than nitrate but can be more toxic, especially in drinking water and aquaculture, because it interferes with oxygen transport in some aquatic organisms.
